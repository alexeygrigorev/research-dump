## 6. Overview body

You can get an AI coding assistant to generate an app. Finishing a useful product asks more of you: choosing the right scope, checking the result, making it understandable, getting feedback, and deciding what to improve.

In this course, you’ll work on one product of your own. It can be a small web app, a useful tool for your work, a service for a community, or an early commercial product. It does not need to contain an AI feature. AI is part of how you build it.

We’ll follow a practical cycle:

**Scope → Build → Test → Improve → Launch → Learn.**

You’ll start with a specific user and one useful workflow. Then you’ll use AI to build and deploy a small version, put it in front of other people, and improve it based on what happens—not just on what the model suggests.

This is a build-with-feedback course, not a collection of tool demonstrations. I’ll personally review every submitted project in group office hours. You’ll receive structured feedback from other participants, test their products, and learn from the decisions behind each review.

By the end, you’ll have a deployed product, evidence from testing and launch attempts, and a clearer next step. The course does not promise revenue or product-market fit. It gives you a structured way to build, share, and learn from a real product.

## 7. What you’ll learn

### Choose a product you can actually finish

Identify a specific user and problem. Define one core workflow, write a short product brief, and decide what to leave out of the first version.

**You’ll create:** A focused MVP specification, acceptance criteria, and a plan for finding potential testers.

### Build and deploy with AI without giving up engineering judgment

Break the work into small tasks, give your coding assistant the right context, inspect changes, and verify the main flow before moving on.

**You’ll create:** A deployed application with a working end-to-end flow, basic checks, and clear setup instructions.

### Make your product understandable to someone who did not build it

Improve the first screen, onboarding, and main task. Give reviewers a safe, free way to test the product and a clear way to report problems.

**You’ll create:** A review-ready MVP and a short first-user testing script.

### Turn feedback into a better product

Separate observed problems from assumptions and feature requests. Use personal and peer reviews to choose your next improvements and explain the decisions you make.

**You’ll create:** An improved version, a prioritized backlog, and a changelog showing what changed and why.

### Measure what happens after you share the app

Track the important steps in the user flow, make errors visible, and understand basic operating costs and limitations. Use evidence to choose the next test.

**You’ll create:** A small measurement plan and the checks needed for a responsible first launch.

### Reach potential users and learn from a real launch

Find the communities and channels relevant to your product. Write clear messages, invite people to try it, and document the response—including what did not work.

**You’ll create:** A launch attempt, an evidence-based report, and a practical next-step plan.

## 12. Course syllabus

### Module 1 — Find the problem and scope the first version

**November 2–8**

Choose the user and the job you want to help them do. Research the problem, identify relevant communities, and define the smallest useful product. Turn the idea into a short specification your coding assistant can work from.

Topics: problem evidence; user job; one core flow; in-scope and out-of-scope decisions; acceptance criteria; early tester discovery.

**Deliverable:** Your product brief, MVP scope, and initial audience plan.

### Module 2 — Build and deploy a thin end-to-end product

**November 9–15**

Use AI to implement a small working slice. Connect the parts needed for your main workflow, check the output, and deploy early rather than leaving infrastructure until the end.

Topics: small implementation tasks; project context; frontend and backend boundaries; persistence where needed; smoke tests; deployment; basic error visibility.

**Deliverable:** A deployed product that completes its core flow with safe test data.

### Module 3 — Make the MVP ready for other people

**November 16–22**

Improve clarity, navigation, and reliability. Remove unfinished distractions, explain the product, and give reviewers a straightforward way to test it.

Topics: first-use experience; loading and error states; free reviewer access; README and demo instructions; feedback questions; a short user test.

**Deliverable:** A review-ready MVP with a working link and testing instructions.

### Midterm project — Submit, test, and review

**November 23–December 6**

Submit your deployed MVP by November 29. Review three peer projects during the review window ending December 6. Receive a personal project review from Alexey and turn the feedback into a focused improvement plan.

**Deliverable:** A tested MVP, review notes, and a prioritized backlog.

### Module 4 — Improve the product based on feedback

**December 7–13**

Group the feedback, identify the important problems, and make a deliberate next iteration. You do not need to implement every suggestion.

Topics: observation versus opinion; bug versus feature request; prioritization; smaller changes; verification; communicating what changed.

**Deliverable:** An improved deployed product and an explanation of your decisions.

### Module 5 — Measure use and prepare for launch

**December 14–20**

Check the key steps in your user flow and make failures visible. Understand the product’s operating limits and prepare a responsible release.

Topics: basic product events; activation and completion; error monitoring; costs and limits; access checks; launch readiness.

**Deliverable:** A measurement plan, basic operational checks, and your launch checklist.

### Holiday break

**December 21–January 3**

There are no required live sessions, project submissions, or peer reviews during the break. You may work on your product or catch up, but the course does not depend on working through the holidays.

### Module 6 — Launch to the people your product is for

**January 4–10**

Share your product through selected relevant channels. Write clear messages, ask people to try it, and record what you learn.

Topics: audience-specific distribution; community rules; direct outreach; product messaging; launch observations; next experiments.

**Deliverable:** A documented launch attempt and evidence to guide your next iteration.

### Final project — Review the product and decide what comes next

**January 11–24**

Submit your final product and report by January 17. Review three final peer projects, receive personal feedback from Alexey, and share your work in the final showcase.

Your report should explain what you built, what changed after feedback, how you tried to reach users, and what the results suggest you should do next.

**Deliverable:** A shipped product, a launch and learning report, and a practical roadmap.

## 14. Frequently asked questions

### How is this different from AI Dev Tools Zoomcamp?

AI Dev Tools covers practical work with coding assistants, including development, testing, and deployment. Product Shipping gives you a longer, supported cycle for your own product: choosing the scope, testing it with people, getting personal and peer reviews, improving it, and learning from a launch.

### Does my product have to use AI?

No. You’ll use AI to help create the product, but the product itself can be a conventional web app, utility, or tool. Choose AI features only when they help solve the user’s problem.

### Do I need to be starting a business?

No. A useful tool for your work, a community service, or an open-source project can be a good fit. We’ll focus on whether someone can use it and what you can learn from sharing it.

### Do I need an idea before joining?

A rough idea helps, but it does not need to be finalized. We’ll use the first module to choose a problem and make the scope realistic.

### Will Alexey personally review my project?

Yes. Every project submitted for the course’s midterm and final review milestones receives personal feedback from Alexey in group office hours. Peer feedback is additional; it does not replace the instructor review.

### Are these private one-to-one reviews?

No. The core format is a group review so that everyone can learn from the discussion. Your project receives direct attention, but the session is shared with the cohort.

### Do I have to make my work public?

You need to provide a version that the course reviewers can access and test. Do not share employer-confidential information, real user data, or secrets. Use a sanitized demo where needed. Publishing recordings or excerpts of your project outside the cohort requires your separate permission.

### What happens during the holiday break?

There are no required sessions, submissions, or peer reviews from December 21 to January 3. You may keep building, but you will not need to work over the holidays to meet a hidden deadline.

### What happens if I cannot attend a project review?

Tell us before the review window so we can arrange how you submit your demo and questions. The review still needs to fit the published milestone window; do not leave coordination until after the final deadline.

### Will there be recordings?

Core teaching sessions are planned to be recorded for the cohort. Project reviews and guest sessions will follow the permissions agreed with the people involved. We’ll confirm access arrangements before the course begins.

### Which tools and stack will we use?

The course teaches a repeatable process rather than requiring you to follow every new tool. We’ll publish one supported demo stack and the setup checklist before the start. You can use alternatives, but stack-specific support may be limited.

### Are AI subscriptions and hosting included?

Budget for the tools and hosting your project needs. Do not assume they are included in tuition. We’ll aim for a small first version and review operating costs before launch.

### Will I finish with customers or revenue?

Those results are not guaranteed. You’ll be expected to build, test, share, and report honestly on your launch attempts. A useful outcome can include discovering that the product or positioning needs to change.

### Are guest experts confirmed?

Not yet. Guest sessions are planned, and names will be announced after confirmation. Your core instruction, project reviews, and peer feedback do not depend on any particular guest.
