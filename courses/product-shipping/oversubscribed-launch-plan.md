## 1. The correction

Do not turn the existing blog archive into a calendar of supposedly new articles. Use it as the source library for useful downloadable working tools. Reserve new articles and webinars for a new decision, observation, experiment, or demonstrated change directly connected to the course.

**Old material becomes a tool. New material shows the tool applied to a real product decision. The course provides the sustained process on the participant's own product.**

The public series is **Product Shipping Decisions**. Its recurring question is: **What should this builder do next to get a small product into someone else's hands—and what evidence would change that decision?**

This is not another AI coding tutorial series, an infrastructure course, an accelerator promising revenue, or a disguised stream of sales pitches. The connection to the paid course should be visible and brief.

## 3. Position the actual course, not a generic expertise category

### Best-fit audience hypothesis

Technical builders who can already work with code and Git, but need help taking a personally meaningful idea or prototype through scoping, a usable release, external feedback, iteration, and a real launch attempt.

Existing member-research notes repeatedly identify the laptop-to-live gap, difficulty choosing one project, uncertainty about AI-generated work, and the need for accountability. This is qualitative evidence from the existing community, not a population estimate or proof of demand at a particular price. [S5]

### What prospects should understand

- The unit of work is **their own continuing product**, not a shared tutorial application.
- The value is judgment, two submitted-project review milestones, peer testing, and follow-through—not simply access to information.
- Products need not contain AI features or be commercial startups.
- Customers, revenue, and product-market fit are not guaranteed.
- AI Dev Tools already covers specifications, building, testing, and deployment. Do not diminish the free course to differentiate this one. [S3][S4]

### A useful message to test

> You may already have enough tutorials. The hard part is deciding what to finish, checking that another person can use it, and choosing the next change. In Product Shipping, we work through those decisions on one product of your own.

Validate the alternative with applicants: what did they actually try instead—building alone, another course, seeking feedback, or doing nothing? Do not assume everyone is blocked by the same thing. [B2][B3]

## 4. Make Oversubscribed the operating structure

The eight principles and five campaign phases in the checklist guide the launch. They are not permission to invent scarcity or treat every subscriber as a buyer. [B1]

| Phase | What we do | Required evidence or output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Plan capacity, audience, and delivery | Define the 15 selected places, bounded direct enrollment, review workload, fit, and actual support promises | Capacity budget, clear participation rules, complete economic terms |
| 2. Build anticipation and gather signals | Show useful decisions; offer compact tools; invite project-stage replies and event registration | Unique relevant prospects and evidence of application, not just impressions |
| 3. Measure demand and release deliberately | Review applications in announced rounds; evaluate fit at the applicant's selected arrangement | Qualified applications, offers, signed terms, and paid seats, recorded separately |
| 4. Follow through on sales and delivery | Explain the offer, follow up appropriately, onboard people, deliver promised reviews | Completed enrollment and visible participant progress |
| 5. Celebrate, learn, and improve | Document results and limitations, get publication consent, improve the next offer, preserve recovery time | Honest cases, a debrief, a better next campaign, and a maintained interest list |

### What exactly is scarce?

The working offer remains:

| Route | Admission | Economics | Capacity treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| $100 | Application; applicant chooses this arrangement | Larger project revenue share; full terms still to be finalized | Within the 15 selected places |
| $799 | Application; applicant chooses this arrangement | Smaller project revenue share; full terms still to be finalized | Within the same 15 selected places |
| $1,599 | Application; applicant chooses this arrangement | No project revenue share | Within the same 15 selected places |
| $3,000 | Direct enrollment without the selection questionnaire | No project revenue share; not an extra-coaching package | Separate, finite delivery capacity |

Do not call the lower-upfront arrangements scholarships or simple discounts. The percentages, covered revenue, duration, cap, ownership boundaries, refunds, and reporting obligations are **not finalized by this document**. Earlier illustrative numbers are not approved terms. Resolve these and verify the payment setup before inviting applicants to make an informed choice.

Use **“15 selected places”**, not “only 15 people can join,” while a direct route exists. Paying more bypasses the questionnaire, not basic prerequisites or the workload of reviewing a project. Set the total maximum after budgeting teaching, preparation, individual review time, and support. Do not make direct enrollment unlimited.

The reference price for selected no-share admission is $1,599. Do not present $3,000 as an artificial crossed-out former price. It is a different admission route.

### What counts as demand?

Record a person's strongest current signal and avoid counting the same person across multiple downloads and events as several prospects.

1. Download or registration: interest.
2. Completed worksheet, project reply, or a relevant question: applied engagement.
3. Suitable application after seeing dates, workload, and the complete selected economic arrangement: qualified demand.
4. Accepted offer: a commitment still subject to terms and payment.
5. Signed required terms plus payment: enrolled.

The checklist's 5× strong / 10× engaged / 100× weak signal multiples and 7–11–4 familiarity idea are heuristics, not conversion rates or requirements. For 15 places, five times capacity would be 75 strong signals; do not claim that 75 forms guarantee enrollment or hold up real sales merely to hit that figure. Use actual qualified-to-paid conversion. [B1]

Operational goal: fill up to 15 selected places with suitable, informed, paid participants by November 15, without reducing the promised delivery quality. Track direct enrollments separately.

### Article briefs

**Oct 16 — A Project I Would Admit to This Cohort—and What I Would Cut First**

Use one concrete existing idea or prototype. Show the intended user, what is already present, the finish line, the features being deferred, and where user contact starts. Compare the original plan with the course-sized version. The new information is the decision on this particular project, not a recap of project-ideation advice. Deliver one completed card. Connect to Module 1 and the course's continuing-product structure. Fallback: clearly label Alexey's own hypothetical rescoping exercise; never invent an applicant.

**Oct 23 — After the Terraform Incident: Which Release Safeguards Can I Demonstrate Today?**

Spend only a short paragraph linking back to the incident. The original article already contains a “what I changed” section, so simply retelling it would fail the novelty test. Show a current, sanitized release-boundary map; identify what can modify code, deploy, access data, and recover it; demonstrate one safely isolated check; record what is still untested. Translate the result into a decision a small-product builder must make before inviting testers. Keep platform-specific implementation as supporting detail. Never make an “agents cannot cause harm” claim. Fallback: **Designing a Release Boundary for a Course Project**, explicitly a worked design rather than a claim about deployed personal infrastructure.

**Oct 30 — The Agent Said “Deployed.” Here Is the User Journey I Checked**

The scheduler note is a useful starting point because it distinguishes a deployment report and a few technical checks from a complete end-user task. Re-run an independent, safe full task on a sanitized environment, such as finding availability, making a test booking, and observing the result. Report what passed, failed, or could not be checked. A fully successful test is publishable; do not manufacture a bug. Explain what evidence is sufficient for a first tester invitation and what it does not prove. Fallback: **What I Would Check Before Calling This Deployment Finished**, labeled as a review of the report and a proposed test plan.

**Nov 6 — What I Changed After Watching Someone Try the Product**

Use the Nov 3 observation and Nov 5 follow-up. Show the task, the actual behavior, competing interpretations, the chosen small change, and the later observation. Do not imply a statistically established conversion improvement from one person. The lesson is judgment and iteration. Keep the participant anonymous unless publication consent explicitly permits attribution. Fallback: explain an unresolved observation and the next test; a false successful before/after is not acceptable.

**Nov 13 — My First-User Plan Starts Before the Product Is Finished**

Work through one real project's audience, current workaround, relevant places to reach people, a precise testing invitation, and the evidence to collect. This is an applied plan, not generic “post on LinkedIn” advice or a claim to have acquired customers. If outreach has happened, report the actual attempts and responses, including none. Link it to the course's required launch attempt and evidence-based next-step plan. Include an appropriate next-cohort CTA after current applications close.

### Webinar format

Use two complementary functions: **decision demonstration** and **review/retest**. Do not prepare two unrelated lectures each week.

For a roughly 40-minute session: introduce the concrete situation and boundary; demonstrate the decision; give attendees one short exercise; take relevant questions; finish with a brief transparent invitation. Free clinics cover at most two or three selected projects. Submission is not a guarantee of review. They do not replace the paid cohort's reviews of submitted milestone projects.

One existing application can support several sessions, but each session must produce a new observation or decision. Use test data, a safe environment, permission to show any external work, and a recorded fallback labeled accurately.

## 11. What to capture for the next launch

At onboarding: current project state, intended user, known blockers, and what the participant wants to finish. During delivery: submitted artifacts, review fulfillment, tested tasks, actual feedback, changes, launch attempts, and remaining uncertainty. Use existing course work instead of inventing extra marketing submissions.

A public case should show **before → intervention → observed result → remaining limitation**. A working product, a clearer scope, or an unsuccessful launch with useful evidence can all be honest cases. Do not substitute completion counts for revenue outcomes or claim earlier Buildcamp projects are outcomes from this new course.

After completion, interview a small mix of successful participants, people who struggled, people who withdrew, and suitable non-buyers. Ask about actual experience and why they acted or did not act. Separate testimonial consent from tuition or revenue-share arrangements. [B3][B5][B8][B9]

Maintain next-cohort interest from the start, but distinguish an email subscriber from an informed potential buyer at the next price. Send occasional useful evidence and a relevant resource, not a prolonged countdown without a real next date.

## 12. Higher prices or more people: use separate tests

Default recommendation: improve the experience and evidence, then test a higher no-share price at roughly the same cohort size. Consider greater headcount only after the review system and support capacity are established. This is a recommendation, not an instruction to raise a price automatically after publishing more posts. [B6][B9][B10]

Before the next offer:

1. Cost instructor preparation, teaching, reviews, asynchronous support, administration, and any contractors.
2. Review satisfaction and actual progress, including people who did not finish.
3. Identify which audience received the most value and why.
4. Publish one clear next-cohort price and scope when the offer is ready; test real applications and paid acceptances rather than a hypothetical willingness-to-pay poll.
5. Keep selected-route, direct-route, and revenue-share demand separate.

Demand for $100 plus a share is not validation of a premium no-share price. Future project revenue is not current course revenue. Preserve signed terms for existing students rather than retroactively changing them.

For scale, document a review standard, train any additional reviewer, test the quality, and budget sufficient review windows. Doubling 15 participants to 30 doubles two submitted milestone reviews from 30 to 60 before considering support. Do not quietly dilute the promise that Alexey personally reviews submitted milestone projects.

Test price and capacity separately where practical. When evidence is weak, improve fit or delivery before increasing traffic.

## 13. The book-to-action checklist

These are selective applications from all twelve book checklists in the repository, plus the relevant personal notes. They are not a claim that every book recommends every tactic or that numerical examples are universal best practices.

| Book / source | Idea retained from the notes | Action in this plan | What we explicitly avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oversubscribed** [B1] | Capacity-first planning; anticipation; differentiated signals; deliberate release; delivery; celebration | Five campaign phases, informed applications, real capacity, continuing evidence after launch | Artificial queues, unlimited direct seats disguised as a 15-person cap, heuristic multiples treated as conversion forecasts |
| **Obviously Awesome** [B2] | Real alternatives → distinctive capabilities → customer value → best-fit audience | Position own-product decisions and repeated reviews against what applicants actually tried | A renamed generic AI course or a claim that free Zoomcamp does not teach deployment |
| **The Mom Test** [B3] | Recent behavior, actual workarounds, commitments rather than compliments | Ask about the last attempt; collect actual worksheet use and paid acceptance; interview non-buyers | “Do you like my idea?” validation and leading testimonial questions |
| **Million Dollar Weekend** [B4] | Reachable customers; specific requests; payment evidence; sustainable repetition and a clear goal | Useful targeted invitations, real enrollment asks, repeatable weekly evidence production | Counting likes as demand; 100 pieces of content before learning from results |
| **Superfans** [B5] | Quick wins, participation, showing the process, member recognition | Useful cards, selected clinics, optional project spotlights, respectful treatment of non-admitted applicants | Unlimited personal attention, compulsory advocacy, or rewards unrelated to the task |
| **Company of One** [B6] | Define enough, protect service quality, improve profitability without automatic headcount growth | Test better pricing and repeatability before expanding enrollment | More participants as the only success measure |
| **The Minimalist Entrepreneur** [B7] | Start with community, solve a concrete problem, build little, market through real work | Reuse the archive, serve relevant existing builders, show real decisions | Building a new quiz app or free mini-course as a launch prerequisite |
| **Zero to Sold** [B8] | Audience/problem first, smallest complete outcome, safe incremental release, reusable support | One working user task; bounded releases; repeated advice becomes a card | Fancy architecture or features before a complete useful path |
| **The SaaS Playbook** [B9] | Diagnose funnel drop-offs, sustainable support economics, clear pricing, evidence from real purchases | Demand by arrangement, channel-to-paid measurement, workload and price review | Importing SaaS churn/LTV assumptions as established cohort-course economics |
| **The E-Myth Revisited** [B10] | Document roles, standards, and repeatable operations | A shared content brief, review standard, follow-up process, and ownership of delivery | Founder heroics as the only way to maintain quality |
| **The Lean Product Playbook** [B11] | Separate problem from solution, test a hypothesis, observe use, iterate | Each new case makes one decision from a fresh observation and checks the next step | Turning feature requests directly into a roadmap or claiming one small test proves product-market fit |
| **Hooked** [B12] | Clear prompt, small useful action, competence, useful accumulated work | Download → fill one part → use the same project card again → optional next-step reminder | Compulsive engagement, artificial streaks, and maximizing time in content rather than shipping |
| **Start Small, Stay Small — personal notes** [B13] | Market and reach matter; measurable goals; explain the product through a tour | A short course-experience tour using one sample project and one review, plus an explicit demand goal | Treating a generic technical demonstration as sufficient explanation of the paid experience |

The course-experience tour should be short and concrete: starting project → feedback on one decision → improved artifact → how the two milestone reviews and peer testing work. Use a labeled example until real first-cohort evidence exists. Explain what is group review versus private coaching and what participants must contribute.

## 14. Weekly operating scorecard and decision rules

| Measure | Interpretation | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Unique relevant prospects by source and stage | Reach among likely-fit builders | Continue channels generating relevant questions and projects, not only impressions |
| Card use, project replies, and submissions | Applied engagement | Use recurring blockers to select the next case |
| Qualified applications by selected arrangement | Informed demand | Diagnose fit, terms, timing, and economic mix separately |
| Offer → agreement → payment | Conversion to enrollment | Follow up appropriately; clarify an obstacle rather than immediately discounting |
| Upfront receipts, refunds, costs, and support hours | Commercial and delivery sustainability | Protect the teaching experience; do not book speculative revenue shares |
| Promised vs completed reviews and participant progress | Reliability of the experience | Fix delivery before scaling promotion |
| Consent-cleared case evidence | Material for the next launch | Publish results with limitations and denominators |
| Next-cohort applications / paid commitments at its actual terms | Support for a higher price or capacity | Change one major variable at a time where practical |

For illustration only, 15 selected enrollments need 30 qualified candidates if 50% accept and pay, or 75 if 20% do. These are arithmetic scenarios, not observed conversion rates. Update from actual results rather than adopting a generic waitlist target.

Maintain a small evidence ledger: source link, asset version, project/example, observation date, whether it is author-observed or agent-reported, permission status, next step, and published link. No unnecessary personal or commercial applicant data belongs in this public repository.

1. Check and reconcile the published offer, questionnaire route, direct checkout, schedule, and support budget. Resolve revenue-share terms before presenting those options as ready to accept.
2. Pick one safe demonstration product and gather a current project brief, release evidence, and a short scope decision.
3. Prepare D1, D3, and D4 from existing sources. Link to the originals and label new additions.
4. Perform the new release audit required for the incident follow-up; otherwise use the design-only fallback.
5. Publish the November session pages early, then prepare the first two bounded demonstrations and the Oct 16 article.
6. Prepare the Oct 19–Nov 1 asynchronous articles, clips, and downloads without creating extra live obligations.
7. Run the announced admission rounds, measure informed demand, and complete onboarding.
8. Continue the evidence series during delivery; capture proof responsibly; evaluate the next price and capacity after the actual experience.
