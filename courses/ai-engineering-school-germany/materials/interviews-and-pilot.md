# Research interviews and practical pilot

Updated: 2026-11-09.

**Prepared protocol, not completed research.** No participants have been interviewed, no pilot has been booked or delivered through this work, and no outcomes are claimed. The first research cycle uses existing channels and materials; it does not require hiring.

## Research cycle

| Period | Proposed work | Founder-time hypothesis |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| 9–13 November 2026 | Publish the form and first post; conduct four interviews | About 4.5 hours |
| 16–20 November | Conduct six more interviews, send one reminder, select a pilot topic and prepare the exercise | About 4.5 hours |
| 24–27 November | Deliver one pilot, conduct two final interviews, review work and write a decision | About 5 hours |

The twelve conversations mean **ten prospective learners and two hiring managers or technical leads**, not twelve learners plus unlimited extra employer calls. If conversations or preparation take longer, reduce scope or explicitly revise the time budget.

One proposed pilot slot is **24 November 2026, 14:00–15:30 Europe/Berlin**. Confirm availability and access before advertising it. This is a proposed operational date, not an existing booking.

The research spending envelope is **€0–300**, a management limit rather than a necessary purchase. Existing tools may be sufficient.

## Recruit across the relevant segments

Invite participants who opted in through the [survey](survey.en.md). Include:

- People who already develop software.
- Adjacent technical workers who write scripts or notebooks.
- Beginners and people starting a career transition.

A possible ten-person interview mix is four experienced developers, three adjacent technical workers and three beginners. This is a sampling proposal, not an observed sample or a statistical design. Adjust it to the responses and make any gaps explicit.

Include some people reached through forwarded local contacts rather than only the most active existing community members. The sample still will not represent the whole German market.

Do not require participants to disclose layoffs publicly or provide employer names, benefit documents, income or private employer repositories. Take notes only for the stated research purpose and keep identifiable records out of the public repository.

## Twenty-minute learner interview

| Minutes | Question or activity | Evidence to capture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 | What is your current role or technical background? What role or practical result do you want next? | Starting point and desired outcome |
| 3–7 | Describe a recent task, interview or job requirement where you lacked a skill. What happened? What did you try? | A specific problem rather than generic enthusiasm |
| 7–10 | What have you already used: tools, courses, colleagues, self-study? What was still missing? | Alternatives and unmet support needs |
| 10–13 | Read the three course cards. Choose the most useful and one that is not suitable. Why? What should you be able to show afterwards? | First-choice outcome and reason |
| 13–16 | Could you actually commit to the proposed mornings and total workload? What would you move or stop? Is April early enough? | Real availability, urgency and trade-offs |
| 16–18 | Which funding/payment route are you considering? What have you already discussed? | Current stage without adjudicating eligibility |
| 18–20 | Would you take a short practical task or attend a specific pilot? Agree one next step if useful. | A concrete action and permission to follow up |

### Useful follow-ups

- “Can you show or describe one concrete example?”
- “What did you do when you got stuck?”
- “What is preventing you from reaching this goal with existing free Zoomcamp materials?”
- “Which part needs feedback from a teacher, rather than another video?”
- “Would this still matter if the course started later than April?”
- “If funding were unavailable, what would you do instead?”

The last question explores alternatives. It must not turn inability to self-pay into a negative judgement about a suitable publicly funded learner.

Avoid presenting the preferred course first and then asking whether it sounds exciting. Ask about actual problems before showing the offers. A verbal “interesting” is useful context, not a confirmed enrolment.

## Employer or technical-lead interview

Use two initial conversations to ask about real evaluation practices. Additional conversations can follow if there is a clear gap.

1. Which tasks would a junior or mid-level engineer actually perform in your team?
2. How do you assess use of coding agents, if at all?
3. Which foundations remain mandatory regardless of AI assistance?
4. What would a candidate need to explain in a code review or debugging exercise?
5. Which project outputs would demonstrate independent judgement?
6. How do expectations differ between AI-assisted development and building AI applications?
7. What language requirements apply to the roles you know?
8. Would you review a sample exercise or anonymised project rubric?

Do not turn a friendly conversation into an unsupported claim that an employer will hire graduates. Record the role and context of the advice without publishing identifiable interview notes unless agreed.

## Select one pilot topic

Use early survey and interview results to choose one coherent audience and task. Do not combine beginners and experienced engineers in a single advanced exercise just to fill seats.

### Pilot A: modifying a real repository with a coding agent

Provide a small prepared repository and a bounded feature request or reproducible bug. Participants:

1. Run the project and reproduce the existing behaviour.
2. Describe the requirement and acceptance criteria.
3. Use an AI coding tool to implement a small change.
4. Run tests and review the proposed diff.
5. Explain what changed and why the checks are sufficient for the task.

Include an intentionally weak suggestion or missed edge case in the discussion, so participants must verify rather than accept a plausible-looking answer.

### Pilot B: improving an AI application

Provide a minimal working RAG or tool-using application with a known failure. Participants inspect the issue, make a limited change and evaluate it against a small fixed set of examples. The discussion covers failure analysis, quality evidence and trade-offs rather than only a successful demo.

### If beginner demand is strongest

Design a separate introductory experiment: run a small programme, explain a simple function, change its behaviour and check the result with AI support. Measure how much help is needed with setup, concepts, debugging and independent explanation. This does not validate a short transition from no programming to an AI-engineer job; it tests a narrower foundation offer.

## Ninety-minute agenda

| Minutes | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 0–10 | Task, environment and intended result |
| 10–25 | Short demonstration |
| 25–60 | Participant work, with observed support needs |
| 60–80 | Review and explanation of selected solutions |
| 80–90 | Feedback, format questions and optional next action |

Invite approximately eight to ten suitable people. If necessary, send a 30–45-minute setup/readiness task in advance. Explain its purpose and offer a way to report setup difficulties.

Use existing teaching assets. A new platform, a large recorded course and paid advertising are not prerequisites for this experiment. AI use is explicitly allowed; participants should be able to understand and defend their results.

## Observation rubric

Score each area from 0 to 2 if a numeric summary is useful:

| Area | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Run and understand the project | Cannot yet run or locate the relevant code | Can do so with substantial help | Runs it and explains the relevant logic |
| Diagnose the change | Cannot connect the task to observed behaviour | Partially identifies the issue or needs guidance | Reproduces the issue and explains the change |
| Verify the result | Accepts output without meaningful checking | Runs some checks with help | Uses relevant tests/checks and notices an incorrect suggestion or edge case |
| Explain judgement | Cannot explain the solution | Gives a partial explanation | Explains a risk, limitation or alternative |

A tentative **5/8** readiness threshold can help distinguish people ready for an advanced cohort from those needing preparation. It is a research hypothesis, not an approved admission standard or a claim of psychometric validity. Review whether the task and rubric unfairly measure familiarity with a particular tool or setup.

Record common difficulties and support time as well as scores. A six-person pilot does not establish how much support an entire funded course will require.

## Feedback after the task

- What part was useful for a real task or job goal?
- Where did you need help?
- What was too easy or too advanced?
- Would the proposed weekly timetable be realistic?
- What would stop you from joining in April?
- Would you take the next diagnostic or funding-information step?

## Evidence thresholds for the next decision

These are management hypotheses for a small first cohort, **not observed results, legal minimums or conversion forecasts**.

| Signal | Working target | Decision supported |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Relevant survey coverage | Around 60–100 Germany-based responses | Directional comparison of segments and constraints |
| Matched prospects for one offer | 15 | Continue defining that programme |
| People completing a concrete next action | 8 | Interest survives a modest effort requirement |
| Pilot attendance | 6 | Observe a small group in practice |
| Participants completing and explaining the task | At least 4 | Refine prerequisites and teaching design |
| Actual launch admissions | Eight suitable paid/funded participants, subject to viable economics and all other gates | Decide whether the first cohort can start |

Smaller but coherent evidence may still be informative. Do not chase response counts at the expense of understanding fit. If fewer than ten matched prospects emerge after two substantive outreach attempts, investigate the bottleneck before substantial spending.

If interest is strongest among beginners, retain that finding and evaluate a suitable foundation programme. If interest mainly requires evenings or recordings, record a format mismatch rather than counting those responses as demand for four mornings.

## Minimal private research tracker

Suggested fields:

- Respondent ID and acquisition channel.
- Location category.
- Technical-readiness segment.
- First-choice learning outcome.
- Preferred start and urgency.
- Actual schedule fit.
- Prerequisite evidence, where volunteered or assessed.
- Funding/payment stage.
- Contact permissions.
- Agreed next action and outcome.

Store personal contact details and identifiable notes separately from the public research archive. Publish only suitable aggregate findings. Do not fabricate a populated tracker: the current research has no collected responses.

## Decision note after the pilot

Complete a one-page note with:

1. Which offer and audience are best supported, and by what observations.
2. Who does not fit the first offer and what their unmet need appears to be.
3. The main prerequisite, timing, language and funding uncertainties.
4. Observed preparation and support work, with limits of the small pilot.
5. The proposed next spending decision and its evidence.
6. What needs reconfirmation in January and February.

November interest may change before April. Reconfirm availability and the applicable funding conditions rather than treating an early expression of interest as a reserved or financed place. The [BA Bildungsgutschein guidance](https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/karriere-und-weiterbildung/bildungsgutschein) explains that individual conditions and the approved provider/course matter.
