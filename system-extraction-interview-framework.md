# The Expert-to-System Interview Method

A normal podcast interview tries to produce an interesting conversation. A **system-extraction interview** tries to produce two things simultaneously:

1. An engaging conversation for the listener.
2. An operator-ready artifact—a procedure, checklist, roadmap, decision tree, diagnostic, or playbook that somebody can actually use.

The podcast discussed in *SYSTEMology* is officially called **Business Processes Simplified Podcast**, created by SYSTEMology. Its description says that the team interviews industry experts and then converts those interviews into customizable procedure templates. Apple currently lists 100 episodes. ([Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/nz/podcast/business-processes-simplified-podcast/id1458198224))

The original Tim Reid episode is episode 46, **“The Process for Running a World-Class Podcast.”** The resulting document is organized into seven stages: research, project setup, interview preparation, conducting the interview, post-production, show notes, and promotion. ([SYSTEMology episode 46](https://www.systemhub.com/pe46-the-process-for-running-a-world-class-podcast/))

Another SYSTEMology episode provides an even clearer picture of the intended artifact. Its document contains:

- Why the process exists
- What the operator needs
- The expert’s steps
- An observable definition of completion

That is essentially the data structure the interview questions need to fill. ([SYSTEMology episode 29](https://www.systemhub.com/pe29-making-header-images-on-canva/))

## The most important principle

**Do not interview somebody “about a topic.” Interview them to produce a defined artifact.**

“Running a coffee shop” is not one system. It is a library of systems:

- Opening the shop
- Calibrating the espresso machine
- Ordering inventory
- Scheduling staff
- Handling complaints
- Closing the shop
- Reviewing weekly cash flow

“Becoming an AI engineer” is not a simple SOP. It is more likely a staged roadmap containing milestones, projects, decision points, feedback loops, and standards for progressing from one stage to another.

Before preparing questions, finish this sentence:

> By the end of this episode, a **[specific type of person]** should be able to **[produce an observable result]** when **[a specific situation occurs]**, under **[important constraints]**, and know they have succeeded when **[completion criteria are met]**.

For example:

> By the end of this episode, a Python developer with no professional machine-learning experience should have a 16-week roadmap for becoming interview-ready for an applied AI engineering role, including weekly outputs, portfolio projects, progress tests, and recovery plans when they get stuck.

Or:

> By the end of this episode, a newly promoted coffee-shop shift manager should be able to open a 40-seat shop safely and completely between 5:30 and 7:00 a.m., even when one employee is absent.

Once that sentence is precise, good questions become much easier to generate.

# 1. Choose the kind of document you are creating

Not every expert’s knowledge should be forced into a numbered SOP.

The published SYSTEMology episodes include linear production processes, recurring review checklists, business-growth stages, diagnostic frameworks, and feedback loops. ([SYSTEMology episode 44](https://www.systemhub.com/pe44-researching-writing-and-publishing-world-class-blog-posts/))

| Document shape | Use it when | What you must extract |
|---|---|---|
| **Workflow or SOP** | Actions normally occur in a repeatable order | Trigger, sequence, roles, tools, handoffs, completion |
| **Checklist** | Order may vary, but nothing important can be missed | Required checks, standards, thresholds, sign-off |
| **Decision playbook** | The correct action depends on the situation | Cues, conditions, branches, trade-offs, escalation |
| **Roadmap** | The result develops over weeks, months, or stages | Stages, milestones, exit criteria, feedback, timing |
| **Diagnostic or audit** | The operator must assess a situation before acting | Questions, scoring, interpretation, recommended actions |
| **Improvement loop** | The process repeats and gets refined | Measure, review, adjust, retest, ownership |
| **System map** | The topic is too broad for one procedure | Major component systems, dependencies, priorities |

This decision changes the interview.

For a workflow, ask, “What happens next?”

For a decision playbook, ask, “What are you noticing, and how does that change your choice?”

For a roadmap, ask, “What evidence shows that someone is ready to advance to the next stage?”

For a diagnostic, ask, “What do you inspect, how do you interpret it, and what action follows each finding?”

# 2. Use the blank-document method to create questions

Before the interview, create the skeleton of the final document—even though most fields are blank.

The skeleton should contain:

1. Title
2. Intended operator
3. Purpose and desired result
4. Trigger or starting situation
5. Scope and exclusions
6. Prerequisites
7. Required inputs and tools
8. Roles and responsibilities
9. Main stages
10. Detailed actions
11. Decision rules
12. Quality standards
13. Failure modes and exceptions
14. Metrics
15. Maintenance and review process

Every blank field becomes an interview question.

| Blank document field | Question it generates |
|---|---|
| Operator | “Who exactly is expected to follow this?” |
| Trigger | “What event tells the operator that it is time to begin?” |
| Prerequisites | “What must already be true before step one?” |
| Inputs | “What information, materials, access, or approvals are required?” |
| Action | “What does the person physically do?” |
| Owner | “Who is accountable for this step?” |
| Decision rule | “How do they choose between the available options?” |
| Standard | “What separates acceptable from unacceptable work?” |
| Exception | “What happens when the normal path cannot be followed?” |
| Output | “What is created or changed by this step?” |
| Completion | “What observable evidence means the process is finished?” |
| Maintenance | “Who reviews and updates this system, and when?” |

This is the real framework for coming up with questions:

> **Design the document first. Ask questions that fill its missing cells.**

Questions should be generated by information gaps, not merely by whatever sounds interesting during the research.

# 3. The EXTRACT interview framework

Use the following seven-part structure.

## E — End state and edge of scope

The first job is to prevent the interview from becoming a general conversation about the industry.

Ask:

1. **What exact result are we teaching today?**
2. Who is the person expected to use this method?
3. What should that person already know?
4. What situation or event triggers this process?
5. Where exactly does the process begin?
6. Where does it end?
7. What is outside the scope of today’s process?
8. When would this method not be appropriate?
9. What constraints are we assuming—time, budget, team size, tools, experience, or market?
10. What does a minimally acceptable result look like?
11. What does an exceptional result look like?
12. **What observable evidence tells us that the process is complete?**

A useful opening script is:

> By the end of this conversation, I want a capable listener to be able to achieve **[result]** without needing you in the room. We’ll reconstruct one real example, map the stages, and then stop at the important actions, decisions, tools, and exceptions. Is that the right outcome for this conversation?

This tells the guest that the host will occasionally interrupt vague explanations and ask for operational detail.

## X — eXample before explanation

Experts often give an idealized description when asked, “How do you usually do this?” They describe what should happen, omit small actions, and unconsciously jump over things that have become automatic.

Instead, anchor the interview in a real instance.

Ask:

1. **Think about the most recent time you did this successfully. Can we use that as our example?**
2. What was the situation?
3. Who was involved?
4. What were the stakes?
5. What event made you realize that action was required?
6. What information, files, screens, materials, or tools did you have in front of you?
7. **What did you actually do first?**
8. What happened immediately after that?
9. What did you personally do, as opposed to what the team did?
10. Where did the real process differ from the ideal process?
11. Was there a moment when you changed your plan?
12. What caused you to change it?
13. What did you do that you would probably forget to mention if I had only asked for your general approach?
14. Can you also think of a time when this went badly or became unusually difficult?

This approach is supported by cognitive-task-analysis methods. The Critical Decision Method starts with a real incident, reconstructs it from beginning to end, builds a timeline, divides it into phases or decision points, and then probes the expert’s thinking within each phase. Applied Cognitive Task Analysis was similarly designed to extract the mental demands and skills behind proficient performance and translate them into practical products. ([AHRQ: Critical Decision Method](https://digital.ahrq.gov/health-it-tools-and-resources/evaluation-resources/workflow-assessment-health-it-toolkit/all-workflow-tools/critical-decision-method))

## T — Timeline and top-level map

Do not immediately spend fifteen minutes exploring step one. First obtain the whole map.

Ask:

1. **If you had to divide this into three to seven major stages, what would they be?**
2. What result is produced by each stage?
3. What must happen before stage one?
4. What must happen after the final stage?
5. Which stages must happen sequentially?
6. Which can happen in parallel?
7. Where are the handoffs between people or teams?
8. Where are the waiting periods?
9. Where does the process sometimes loop backward?
10. How long does each stage normally take?
11. Which stage varies the most?
12. Which stage is hardest to teach?
13. Which stage carries the greatest risk?
14. Which stage do beginners tend to underestimate?

At this point, repeat the stages back to the guest:

> So the process appears to be: first X, then Y, then Z. Is that the correct top-level map before we go into the details?

This prevents discovering late in the interview that the guest forgot an entire phase.

## R — Repeatable details for every stage

Return to stage one and work through every stage using the same data structure.

For each stage, ask:

1. **What is the purpose of this stage?**
2. What triggers it?
3. Who owns it?
4. Who else is involved?
5. What inputs must be available?
6. **What does the person actually do?**
7. Can you break that action into observable verbs?
8. What tool, template, checklist, prompt, script, or setting do they use?
9. Can you give us the exact words you say or write?
10. What information do they enter?
11. Where does that information come from?
12. How long should this normally take?
13. How frequently does it happen?
14. Which parts are mandatory?
15. Which parts are optional?
16. What output does this stage produce?
17. Where is that output saved or recorded?
18. Who receives it next?
19. What must be communicated during the handoff?
20. **What small action is obvious to you but would not be obvious to a beginner?**

The internal data model is:

> **Trigger → Owner → Input → Action → Tool → Rule → Output → Handoff → Exception**

Do not necessarily ask that as one giant question. Ask one short question at a time, but keep checking whether all nine fields have been filled.

A strong master probe is:

> When this situation occurs, who does what, using which inputs or tools, according to what rule, until what observable result is produced—and what happens when the normal path fails?

## A — Adaptive judgment

A conventional SOP captures visible actions. A useful expert playbook must also capture invisible judgment.

This is usually the most valuable part of the interview.

Ask:

1. **Where in this process does the operator have to make a judgment rather than simply follow instructions?**
2. What are they looking at when making that judgment?
3. What cues or signals do you notice?
4. Which cues matter most?
5. What thresholds cause you to take a different action?
6. What are the available options at that point?
7. Which option is the normal default?
8. Under what conditions would you choose another option?
9. What alternatives did you consider in the example we discussed?
10. Why did you reject those alternatives?
11. What trade-off are you making?
12. What is your rule of thumb?
13. What would make you break your own rule?
14. **What would an experienced person notice here that a beginner would miss?**
15. What does a beginner tend to focus on that is actually unimportant?
16. How would your approach change with half the budget?
17. How would it change with ten times the volume?
18. How would it change for a beginner versus an experienced operator?
19. How would it change when time is limited?
20. Where might another credible expert disagree with your approach?
21. What assumptions would have to change for that other approach to be better?

When the guest says, **“It depends,”** that is not a bad answer. It means a decision table has been discovered.

Ask:

> What, specifically, does it depend on?

Then identify the variables:

- Condition A → action A
- Condition B → action B
- Condition C → escalate
- Missing information → gather more information first

## C — Controls and contingencies

Most weak how-to interviews capture only the happy path. A real operating system must explain how quality is controlled and what happens when reality does not cooperate.

Ask:

1. **How does the operator verify that this step was done correctly?**
2. What evidence should exist?
3. Is there a checklist or approval?
4. Who reviews the work?
5. What standard must be met before the process can continue?
6. What metric matters?
7. What threshold is acceptable?
8. What threshold indicates a problem?
9. What are the three most common ways this process fails?
10. Which failure is most expensive or dangerous?
11. What usually causes each failure?
12. **How can the failure be prevented upstream?**
13. What is the earliest warning sign?
14. How should the operator recover after the failure occurs?
15. At what point should they stop rather than continue?
16. When should they escalate to someone more experienced?
17. Who should receive the escalation?
18. What information should accompany it?
19. Are there safety, legal, financial, ethical, or reputational boundaries?
20. What unusual edge case should be included in the document?
21. How often should this system be reviewed?
22. Who owns the current version?
23. What event should trigger an immediate update?

This prevention-oriented questioning appears clearly in *Process Breakdown*. The host moves from a concrete client case to the first action, level of detail, highest-risk failures, upstream prevention, stop gates, exact customer wording, common mistakes, tools, and the first action listeners should take. ([SweetProcess: How to create SOPs and reduce bottlenecks](https://www.sweetprocess.com/how-to-create-sops-and-reduce-process-bottlenecks/))

## T — Teach-back and test

Do not end after the guest finishes explaining. Validate what was captured.

Say:

> Let me explain the process back to you as though I were teaching it to someone else.

Then summarize the trigger, stages, principal decisions, outputs, and completion criteria.

Ask:

1. **What did I misunderstand?**
2. What did I leave out?
3. Did I put anything in the wrong order?
4. Where would a smart beginner still get stuck?
5. Which instruction is still too vague?
6. What would someone be most likely to misinterpret?
7. Let’s test the process: suppose **[realistic complication]** happens. What should the operator do?
8. What happens if the expected input is missing?
9. What happens if there is not enough time?
10. What happens if two rules appear to conflict?
11. **What is the first useful action a listener can take in the next 30 minutes?**
12. What template, example, script, screenshot, or sample output would make this much easier to use?
13. What should the listener not attempt without additional supervision?
14. Will you review the resulting document for factual accuracy?

This last phase is essential because an articulate explanation can still contain invisible gaps.

SYSTEMology’s own system-creation guidance recommends reviewing the draft during the next real use and teaching it to somebody who has not previously performed the task; confusion and missing steps then become visible. ([SYSTEMology: Free SOP Templates](https://www.systemhub.com/free-sop-templates/))

# 4. Rescue probes to use when an answer is vague

Certain words signal that operational information is missing.

| The guest says | Follow-up |
|---|---|
| “Prepare everything.” | “What exactly must be prepared, and what does preparation look like?” |
| “Optimize it.” | “Which variable do you change, based on what data, and toward what target?” |
| “Make sure it is good.” | “How do you inspect it, and what criteria separate acceptable from unacceptable?” |
| “Use your judgment.” | “What cues are you noticing, and what rule converts those cues into a decision?” |
| “It depends.” | “On which two or three variables does it depend?” |
| “Do some research.” | “Where do you look, what do you search for, and what information do you record?” |
| “Choose the best option.” | “What are the options, and how do you compare them?” |
| “Follow up appropriately.” | “After how long, through which channel, and using what wording?” |
| “As needed.” | “What event or threshold creates the need?” |
| “Usually.” | “What is the default path, and what exceptions change it?” |
| “High quality.” | “Can you show us one acceptable and one unacceptable example?” |
| “Communicate with the team.” | “Who contacts whom, through which channel, saying what, and by when?” |

Other powerful rescue questions:

- What would I physically see someone doing?
- What would be open on their screen?
- What is the exact output?
- What happens immediately before that?
- What happens immediately after?
- Can you give me the exact wording?
- Can you give me a number or threshold?
- Tell me about the last time that happened.
- How would a beginner get this wrong?
- Could somebody follow that instruction without asking you another question?

A useful host phrase is:

> That gives us the principle. Now let’s make it executable.

# 5. How to keep the podcast interesting

There is a tension here. A perfectly exhaustive SOP can make terrible audio. A fascinating conversation can make an unusable SOP.

Solve this by designing **two different outputs from the same interview**.

## The audio episode

Prioritize:

- Stories
- Stakes
- Surprising decisions
- Costly mistakes
- Trade-offs
- Examples
- Expert judgment
- Counterintuitive lessons

## The companion system

Prioritize:

- Prerequisites
- Exact steps
- Roles
- Tools
- Settings
- Scripts
- Decision tables
- Quality criteria
- Troubleshooting
- Checklists

During the recording, use this repeating rhythm:

> **Story → Stage → Specific action → Decision rule → Exception**

For example:

1. Tell us about a time this went badly.
2. Which stage of the process failed?
3. What should the operator do at that stage?
4. How do they decide whether to continue?
5. What should they do when the normal option is unavailable?

That gives the audience emotion and narrative while also generating documentation.

A strong 60-minute run of show:

| Time | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 0–5 minutes | A real success, failure, or high-stakes story |
| 5–10 minutes | Define the operator, result, scope, and completion criteria |
| 10–15 minutes | Map the three to seven major stages |
| 15–42 minutes | Deep-dive through the stages |
| 42–50 minutes | Decisions, exceptions, and a failed case |
| 50–56 minutes | Tools, templates, metrics, and quality controls |
| 56–60 minutes | Teach-back, first action, and final correction |

Useful questions for generating memorable moments include:

- What did this mistake cost?
- What commonly accepted advice do you disagree with?
- Which step seems unimportant but changes the result?
- What do people make unnecessarily complicated?
- What is the most counterintuitive decision in your method?
- When did you discover that your old method was wrong?
- What would you still never automate?
- What does your best operator do differently from everyone else?

Process-oriented shows that remain interesting often combine concrete examples with operational boundaries. *Operations with Sean Lane*, for example, moves from defining a concept to real client examples, ownership and handoff boundaries, and how the team attacks a particular operational problem. ([Operations with Sean Lane transcript](https://www.operationspodcast.com/episodes/demystifying-go-to-market-efficiency-with-go-nimbly-ceo-jen-igartua/transcript))

# 6. Example: interviewing someone about becoming an AI engineer

“Becoming an AI engineer” is too broad for a conventional SOP. The output should probably be a **roadmap plus decision playbook**.

First define the artifact:

> A 16-week roadmap for an employed Python developer who can study ten hours per week and wants to qualify for an applied AI engineering role, with projects, milestones, assessments, and job-readiness criteria.

Then ask:

## Scope

- What kind of AI engineer are we preparing for: model research, ML engineering, LLM applications, data engineering, or AI product engineering?
- What baseline skills must the learner already have?
- What target role and company type are we assuming?
- What is deliberately excluded from this roadmap?

## Stages

- What are the four to six stages between their current level and being interview-ready?
- What tangible output should they produce in each stage?
- How many weeks should each stage take?
- What must they demonstrate before advancing?

## Learning actions

- What should they build rather than merely read about?
- What should the first portfolio project be?
- What should the second project prove that the first does not?
- What tools should they learn deeply, and which only superficially?
- What should they practice every week?

## Judgment and branches

- How should they choose between specializing in LLM applications and traditional ML?
- What signals indicate that they need more mathematics?
- What signals indicate that they are spending too much time on theory?
- What should they do when they cannot complete a project?
- When should they ask for feedback rather than continue independently?

## Quality and proof

- What would make a portfolio project impressive to a hiring manager?
- What makes a project look like a tutorial copied from the internet?
- What tests, documentation, deployment, and monitoring must be included?
- What evidence demonstrates that the candidate can work on a production system?
- What should they be able to explain without notes?

## Failure and recovery

- Where do most learners drop out?
- What do they over-study?
- What do they avoid because it is uncomfortable?
- What is the recovery plan after falling two weeks behind?
- At what point should they revise the target role?

Now the interview can become a useful roadmap rather than a collection of “learn Python, study machine learning, build projects” platitudes.

# 7. Example: interviewing someone about running a coffee shop

“Running a coffee shop” should first become a **system map interview**.

Ask:

1. What are the eight to twelve recurring systems required to operate a coffee shop?
2. Which systems directly affect customer experience?
3. Which systems carry the greatest safety or financial risk?
4. Which are daily, weekly, monthly, and exceptional?
5. Which depend heavily on the owner?
6. Which should be documented first?
7. Where are the handoffs between the front of house, baristas, kitchen, manager, and owner?

That produces the podcast series.

Then choose one system, such as **opening the shop**:

- What event officially begins the opening procedure?
- Who must arrive first?
- What is the first safety check?
- Which equipment must be turned on immediately because it requires warm-up time?
- Which tasks can happen in parallel?
- How is the espresso recipe calibrated?
- What measurement makes the calibration acceptable?
- How is the till float verified?
- What stock levels must be checked?
- What happens if a delivery did not arrive?
- What happens if the espresso machine fails?
- Who has authority to delay opening?
- What must be recorded before the doors open?
- What final walk-through proves that the shop is customer-ready?
- Who signs off?

That conversation can be converted directly into an opening checklist, role assignment, quality-control table, and equipment-failure playbook.

# 8. Send the guest a preparation brief

The guest should know in advance that this will not be a normal promotional interview.

A simple message:

> We are going to build one practical system during the interview rather than discuss the subject generally. Please choose one specific result that you produce repeatedly. Bring one recent successful example, one difficult or failed example, and any templates, checklists, scripts, screenshots, sample outputs, metrics, or tools that support the method. We will first map the major stages and then examine the actions, decisions, quality standards, and exceptions within each stage.

Ask the guest to answer these questions before the recording:

1. What specific result will your system produce?
2. Who is it for?
3. What triggers it?
4. What are its three to seven major stages?
5. Which stages require the most expert judgment?
6. What are the most common failures?
7. What tools or templates should listeners receive?
8. What recent case can we reconstruct?

This preparation alone will substantially improve the episode.

# 9. The document to create after the interview

Use this final structure.

## System title

“How to **[produce result]** when **[trigger/context]**”

## Purpose

Why this system exists and what it produces.

## Intended operator

Who should use it and what they should already know.

## Scope

What the system covers and does not cover.

## Trigger

The event or condition that starts the process.

## Definition of done

The observable evidence that the desired result has been achieved.

## Prerequisites and inputs

Access, information, skills, approvals, tools, materials, and previous outputs.

## Roles

Primary owner, supporting roles, approver, and escalation contact.

## Tools and assets

Software, equipment, templates, scripts, checklists, examples, and reference material.

## Procedure

For each step, document:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Step | Short, action-oriented name |
| Trigger/input | What must be available |
| Owner | Person or role responsible |
| Action | Exact observable behavior |
| Tool or asset | Template, script, software, equipment |
| Rule or standard | How decisions and quality are judged |
| Output | What this step produces |
| Handoff | Who receives the output |
| Exception | What to do when the standard path fails |

## Decision table

| Condition or cue | Interpretation | Action | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|

## Quality checklist

Observable criteria that must be true before completion.

## Troubleshooting

Problem, likely cause, early warning, corrective action, and stop condition.

## Metrics

Measures, thresholds, frequency, and owner.

## Governance

Document owner, version, approval date, review frequency, and change history.

## Source evidence

Relevant transcript timestamps, examples, templates, and supporting references.

# 10. Turning the transcript into the system

When reviewing the transcript, extract four kinds of information.

## Verbs

These become actions:

- Research
- Compare
- Approve
- Send
- Measure
- Record
- Escalate

## Nouns

These become roles, inputs, tools, and outputs:

- Shift manager
- Client brief
- CRM
- Checklist
- Approval email
- Weekly report

## “If” statements

These become decision rules and exceptions:

- If payment has not arrived…
- If the test result is below the threshold…
- If no qualified candidate remains…
- If the machine cannot be calibrated…

## Numbers

These become operational standards:

- Within 24 hours
- Above 80 percent
- Three follow-ups
- Ten hours per week
- A 50 percent deposit
- Review every quarter

Then:

1. Build the top-level map.
2. Convert each stage into step cards.
3. Separate the default path from exceptions.
4. Mark missing information as **[MISSING]**.
5. Mark conflicting statements as **[CONFLICT]**.
6. Mark your own interpretation as **[ASSUMPTION]**.
7. Ask the guest only the unresolved questions.
8. Test the document with a beginner.
9. Update it after the first real use.

Do not silently invent missing steps merely because they seem obvious.

# 11. A transcript-to-system AI prompt

Use the following after obtaining a timestamped transcript:

```text
You are a process analyst. Convert the attached expert interview into an
operator-ready system.

Use only information explicitly stated in the transcript. Do not invent
actions, tools, standards, or decision rules.

Produce:

1. System title
2. Purpose and desired result
3. Intended operator and required skill level
4. Trigger
5. Scope and exclusions
6. Definition of done
7. Prerequisites and inputs
8. Roles and responsibilities
9. Tools, templates, scripts, and examples
10. Three-to-seven-stage process map
11. Detailed procedure

For every procedure step, include:
- trigger or input
- owner
- exact action
- tool or asset
- decision rule
- quality standard
- output
- handoff
- expected timing
- exceptions

Also produce:
- decision table
- quality-control checklist
- failure-prevention and troubleshooting table
- metrics and thresholds
- escalation rules
- maintenance and review instructions
- list of promised supporting assets

Cite the relevant transcript timestamp for every substantive instruction.

When information is absent, write [MISSING].
When two statements conflict, write [CONFLICT].
When an instruction is ambiguous, write [CLARIFY] and formulate the exact
follow-up question that should be sent to the guest.

Finish with:
A. the ten most important follow-up questions;
B. the three places where a beginner is most likely to get stuck;
C. a one-page quick-start checklist.
```

# Core mindset

Do not primarily ask:

> What does this expert know?

Reconstruct:

> **What does a competent operator do, in what order, with which tools, based on which cues and rules, to produce what result—and how do they recognize and recover from failure?**

That shift—from interviewing for opinions to interviewing for executable behavior—is what turns a podcast episode into a genuine system.
