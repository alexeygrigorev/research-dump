# Obviously Awesome — Action Checklist

**April Dunford · English reading/listening companion · Summary-based, revision-aware edition**

Practical actions reconstructed from summaries of this specific book and the author's explanations. This is not an official worksheet or a verified inventory of every instruction in the full text. Wording, task splitting, and the navigation headings are editorial.

**Edition note:** the cover supplied for this checklist says “Updated and Expanded.” Dunford describes the revised method as preparation, five core components, and follow-through, rather than ten equally weighted steps. Accordingly, this checklist uses that structure and gives an original-edition step crosswalk. It does not claim to reproduce the revised edition's exact table of contents. Items marked **Update** reflect her written explanations of the revision. [A17] [A18]

**Use:** mark completed actions with `- [x]`. **Practice** is an action; **Reflection** is a decision to work through; **Option** is an alternative, not an additional requirement. Work through the five core components in order. References beside tasks link to their evidence; source notes identify summaries versus author material.

## Foundations — Understand the context you are creating

- [ ] **Reflection:** Separate positioning decisions from your tagline, advertising copy, mission, and long-term vision. [AQ]
- [ ] **Practice:** Write down what customers currently assume your product is when they first encounter it. [AQ]
- [ ] **Practice:** List the competitors, features, price level, and buyers that this assumed category makes them expect. [AQ]
- [ ] **Practice:** Look for positioning warning signs: confused prospects, slow decisions, price pressure, or customers who never see the value. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice:** Audit whether your branding, messaging, pricing, partnerships, and product experience suggest the same context. [AN]
- [ ] **Reflection:** Identify assumptions hidden inside any positioning statement you already use; investigate them rather than merely filling its blanks. [AQ]

## Preparation — Set up the exercise

### Decide the scope and buying perspective — revised-edition preparation

- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Decide exactly what you are positioning: one product, a lead offering, a suite, or a platform. [A17]
- [ ] **Reflection — Update:** For a single-product business, check whether product and company positioning are effectively the same decision. [A17]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Identify the internal champion who would advocate for buying your product. [A17]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Separate what customers can buy today from the future story you might tell investors. [A17]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Before launch, label your positioning a hypothesis to test, not a proven conclusion. [A17]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Keep that prelaunch hypothesis flexible enough to change with customer evidence. [A17]

### Choose the customer evidence — original step 1

- [ ] **Practice:** Identify customers who quickly understood the product and became enthusiastic users. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Include customers willing to recommend you or act as references. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Learn what these customers value, rather than treating every account's preferences as equally informative. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Collect concrete examples of how they use the product and what outcome matters to them. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Identify obvious poor-fit customers so they do not determine the exercise's direction. [A18]

### Assemble the working group — original step 2

- [ ] **Practice:** Involve sales, product, marketing, and the relevant founder or executive decision-maker. [A18]
- [ ] **Practice:** Secure a strategic leader's participation and commitment to the resulting decisions. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Keep the group small enough to decide, while retaining the necessary customer and product perspectives. [A18]
- [ ] **Option:** Use an experienced facilitator when the team needs help challenging assumptions and reaching agreement. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice:** Treat positioning as a cross-functional decision, not a marketing assignment handed off after completion. [A18]

### Agree on language and release old assumptions — original step 3

- [ ] **Practice:** Agree what the team means by positioning, alternatives, attributes, value, and market category. [A18]
- [ ] **Practice:** Identify labels inherited from the product's original idea that may no longer fit. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Invite the team to consider a different context without defending past decisions. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Ground the discussion in the product's current strengths rather than its original intended identity. [AW]

## Core component 1 — The alternatives customers actually consider

*Original step 4.*

- [ ] **Practice:** Ask what customers would do without your product. [AQ]
- [ ] **Practice:** Include manual work, spreadsheets, internal solutions, and doing nothing when those are genuine choices. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Collect evidence of the alternatives appearing on buyers' current shortlists. [A19]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Bring recent sales experience into the discussion instead of relying only on a market map. [A19]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Remove imagined threats that buyers do not seriously consider today. [A19]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Check whether each department's perspective is distorting the competitive picture. [A19]
- [ ] **Practice:** Group similar alternatives so the team can compare meaningful categories of choice. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Agree on these alternatives before discussing differentiated capabilities or an aspirational category. [A19]

## Core component 2 — Capabilities that make you different

*Original step 5.*

- [ ] **Practice:** Compare your product with the agreed alternatives, not with every theoretically competing product. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Brainstorm distinctive capabilities before dismissing items that initially seem unimportant. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Include relevant company capabilities as well as product features. [AW]
- [ ] **Practice:** Reconsider apparently negative attributes that might benefit a particular customer. [AW]
- [ ] **Practice:** Replace vague claims such as superior quality with specific, demonstrable differences. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Attach evidence to important claims, including independent validation where available. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Retain differences that matter relative to a real alternative, even when they are not globally unique. [AN]

## Core component 3 — Translate differences into customer value

*Original step 6; expanded treatment of differentiated value in the revision.*

- [ ] **Practice:** Connect each distinctive capability to an outcome a customer can experience. [A20]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Keep asking why the difference matters until you reach a meaningful customer result. [A20]
- [ ] **Practice:** Distinguish the feature, its immediate benefit, and the customer's larger objective. [AW]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Replace generic savings claims with a specific explanation of your differentiated outcome. [A20]
- [ ] **Practice:** Group capabilities that produce the same customer benefit into a value theme. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Reduce the themes to a small, memorable set without concealing genuinely different benefits. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Express value from the champion's perspective rather than trying to satisfy every stakeholder at once. [A20]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Agree on the substance of the value before polishing the wording. [A20]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Keep differentiated value separate from answers to ordinary buying objections. [A20]

## Core component 4 — Identify who cares most

*Original step 7.*

- [ ] **Practice:** For each value theme, identify the customers who would care disproportionately about it. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Describe their recognizable characteristics beyond generic industry or company-size labels. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Consider workflow, existing technology investments, and relevant skills as possible identifying traits. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice:** Check whether those traits can help you find and qualify prospects in practice. [AN]
- [ ] **Practice:** Narrow the segment when doing so makes the differentiated value more compelling. [AW]
- [ ] **Practice:** Check that the segment still contains enough reachable business for your near-term objectives. [AW]
- [ ] **Reflection:** Identify whom you will deliberately not target with this positioning. [AR]

## Core component 5 — Choose a category that makes your value clear

*Original step 8. The three positioning styles below are alternatives.*

- [ ] **Practice:** Choose the category after clarifying alternatives, capabilities, value, and best-fit customers. [AQ]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Test what buyers would naturally expect from each candidate category. [A21]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Reject a category that creates misleading expectations about what the product does. [A21]
- [ ] **Option:** Start with an obvious existing category when it immediately makes the product understandable. [AW]
- [ ] **Option:** Explore an adjacent category that better highlights the value you already deliver. [AW]
- [ ] **Option:** Compete broadly in an established category when you have a credible advantage against its alternatives. [AW]
- [ ] **Option:** Target a specific underserved segment within an existing category. [AW]
- [ ] **Option — Update:** Consider creating a category only when the product needs it and you can support substantial market education. [A21]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Use the category to orient the buyer; do not expect its name to explain all your differentiation. [A21]

## Follow-through — Turn the positioning into usable decisions

### Decide whether a trend helps — original step 9, optional

- [ ] **Reflection:** Identify a relevant trend that could explain why your product matters now. [AR]
- [ ] **Option:** Connect the trend to a real product capability and your chosen market context. [AH]
- [ ] **Practice:** Omit the trend when it adds fashionable language but weakens clarity. [AR]

### Record and share the outcome — original step 10

- [ ] **Practice:** Capture the product, category, alternatives, differentiators, value themes, and best-fit customer traits together. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** Make the result concise enough for colleagues to use as a shared reference. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Share the positioning across functions rather than leaving it in the workshop notes. [AR]
- [ ] **Practice:** Review what the positioning implies for product priorities and pricing. [AT]

### Build the sales story — original execution section

- [ ] **Practice:** Open with the customer's problem in language the intended buyer recognizes. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** Explain the available approaches and the limitations that matter to this customer. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** Describe the better outcome and the capabilities needed to achieve it. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** Introduce your product in the chosen category and connect it to those capabilities. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** Support the important value themes with relevant evidence rather than an exhaustive feature tour. [AT]
- [ ] **Practice:** End with a clear next step for the buyer. [AT]

### Test, implement, and revisit

- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Test the positioning in live sales conversations with best-fit prospects. [A22]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Do not interpret a homepage A/B result as a clean positioning test; other variables can change the outcome. [A22]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Create a common messaging document before rewriting the homepage. [A22]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Apply the agreed language consistently across sales materials, campaigns, events, and the website. [A22]
- [ ] **Practice — Update:** Review product, competitor, and market changes regularly; revisit positioning when the evidence warrants it. [A22]

## Coverage and source notes

This combines detailed original-edition summaries with the author's public explanations of the revision. It is not a claim that every revised-edition exercise has been recovered. The original step labels are a crosswalk, not extra tasks to complete again.

The author-series sources below are **written episode descriptions and timestamped outlines**, not claims to have listened to the episodes or read unavailable transcripts. Her gated workbook was not used. Sales-story actions here are supported by the Obviously Awesome summaries; no separate Sales Pitch checklist has been imported.

No fixed interview count, workshop duration, publishing quota, or mandatory trend was added. Signs of weak positioning are prompts for investigation, not proof that positioning is the sole cause of a business problem.
