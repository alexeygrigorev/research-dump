## Core idea

The key difference is the order of operations.

A conventional event process often looks like:

1. Define the conference, venue and programme.
2. Commit money and organizational effort.
3. Announce tickets.
4. Try to persuade a broad audience to buy.

The alternative described through Priestley’s *Oversubscribed* logic is:

1. Define the audience and the problem the event is meant to solve.
2. Start a conversation with potential attendees before the event is fully locked.
3. Ask for low-commitment signals of interest, such as a waiting-list registration.
4. Use those signals to refine the event and estimate real demand.
5. Build trust and clarity through useful content and repeated contact.
6. Make large irreversible commitments only after demand is better understood.
7. Open sales to a prepared audience at a clear moment.

The practical goal is not “create hype.” It is to **reduce uncertainty on both sides before asking for a hard commitment**.

## Useful operating principle

Treat pre-launch audience building as both **marketing and product discovery**:

- The audience learns what the event is for, who it is for, and what outcome it promises.
- The organizer learns which topics, formats, dates, prices and constraints actually matter.
- A waiting list is evidence of interest, but not equivalent to paid demand.
- Stronger signals — e.g. willingness to attend at a known price/date — should carry more weight than passive engagement.
- Scarcity should be real rather than manufactured.

For a new conference, define a checkpoint before committing most of the budget: by a specific date, require a minimum number and quality of demand signals; otherwise change the format, postpone, shrink, or stop.

## Main takeaway

**A conference should ideally become the next step in a conversation that is already happening with the audience, rather than the first reason to contact them.**
